How to get a barcode for your product

To sell a product in shops or on marketplaces, you need two things: a barcode number that is officially registered to your business, and a barcode image of that number on the packaging. Here's how to get both.

The short answer

  1. Get a registered number from GS1, the non-profit that runs the worldwide barcode numbering system, through the GS1 member organisation in your country.
  2. Assign one number to each product (every size, colour or flavour gets its own).
  3. Turn the number into a barcode image with a barcode generator and put it on your label or packaging.
  4. Test it with a scanner or phone app before printing a large run.

The rest of this guide explains each step and the mistakes that most often get products rejected by retailers.

The barcode number is what matters, not the image

A barcode is just a picture of a number. Any barcode generator, ours included, will draw a valid-looking barcode from any digits that pass the check digit calculation. What a generator can't do is make that number yours.

Behind retail barcodes sits a global system called GTIN (Global Trade Item Number). It guarantees that each number belongs to exactly one product from one company. Shops, wholesalers and marketplaces look up the number to find out who made the product. If you invent a number, it may already belong to someone else's product, and retailers can refuse to list it.

How many digits does a barcode number have?

FormatDigitsWhere it's used
EAN-13 (GTIN-13)13Retail products in Europe and most of the world
UPC-A (GTIN-12)12Retail products in the USA and Canada
EAN-8 (GTIN-8)8Very small packages with no room for a full code
GTIN-1414Outer cartons and cases, not single items

An EAN-13 number is made of three parts: a company prefix assigned to your business by GS1 (it starts with a code for the GS1 office that issued it), an item reference that you choose for each product, and a final check digit calculated from the other twelve. Not sure which format you need? See EAN-13, UPC-A or CODE128.

Step 1: get a number from your national GS1 organisation

GS1 has a member organisation in more than 100 countries, and you apply to the one where your business is based. You can find yours in the directory on the GS1 website (gs1.org). For example, GS1 US covers the USA, GS1 UK the United Kingdom, GS1 Ireland the Republic of Ireland, and GS1 Slovakia covers Slovakia.

The process is similar everywhere:

  1. Apply online on your national GS1 organisation's website.
  2. Choose how many numbers you need. Plans are usually sold in blocks (for example 10, 100 or 1,000 numbers), so estimate how many products and variants you'll have over the next few years.
  3. Pay the fee. Prices depend on the country and the number of codes, and most organisations charge a joining fee plus an annual renewal. Check the current price list on your GS1 organisation's website.
  4. Receive your company prefix and start assigning numbers to your products.

Some GS1 organisations, such as GS1 US, also sell individual GTINs for businesses that need only one or a few codes, without a full company prefix.

Be careful with cheap barcode resellers

Many websites sell single barcode numbers for a few dollars. These are usually numbers taken from old company prefixes that were bought before GS1 tightened its rules. They will scan at a till, but the GS1 database still lists the original company as the owner, not you.

That matters more and more. Amazon and many large retailers check barcode numbers against the GS1 database and can reject listings where the brand doesn't match. If you plan to sell through a major marketplace or retail chain, buy your numbers from GS1 directly. Resold numbers are a gamble that only makes sense for very small, local sales.

Step 2: assign one number per product variant

Each distinct product needs its own GTIN. In practice that means a separate number for every size, colour, flavour and pack size. A 250 ml and a 500 ml bottle of the same drink are two products. A pack of 6 is a third. Keep a simple spreadsheet of which number belongs to which product, and never reuse a number for a different product.

Step 3: create the barcode image

Enter your registered number into a barcode generator: choose EAN-13 for a 13-digit number or UPC-A for a 12-digit one. Our generator is free, runs in your browser and checks the check digit for you. Download the image and place it on your label or packaging artwork.

For reliable scanning, print the barcode in dark ink (ideally black) on a light, plain background, keep the white margin on both sides, and don't shrink it too much. A full-size EAN-13 is about 37 × 26 mm, and many retailers won't accept codes much smaller than that.

Step 4: test before you print

Print a sample at actual size and scan it with a barcode scanner or a phone app. Check that it reads the exact number you assigned. Testing one sheet costs nothing compared to reprinting a thousand labels.

When you don't need a registered barcode

If the code will never pass through a shop till or a marketplace (for example internal stock tracking, asset labels, or products sold only in your own shop with your own system), you don't need GS1 registration at all. For those uses, CODE128 instead of EAN-13 is usually the better format, because it isn't tied to the GTIN system and can also hold letters.

Frequently asked questions

Can I make up my own barcode number?

You can generate an image from any number, but for retail sale you shouldn't. A made-up number may already belong to another company's product, and retailers or marketplaces that check the GS1 database can reject it.

Does the barcode show which country my product is made in?

No. The first digits show which GS1 office issued the company prefix, not where the product was made. A company registered in Slovakia gets numbers starting with 858 even if it manufactures abroad.

Will a barcode from one country work in another?

Yes. GTINs are unique worldwide, so a number registered with any GS1 organisation can be scanned and sold anywhere. Scanners outside North America read 12-digit UPC-A codes too.

Do I need a new barcode if I change the packaging design?

Not for purely cosmetic changes. You need a new number when the product itself changes in a way the buyer or retailer should notice, for example a different size, quantity or recipe. Your GS1 organisation publishes detailed rules.
